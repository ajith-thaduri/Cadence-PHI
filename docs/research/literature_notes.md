# Literature notes: small local clinical PHI de-identification (Presidio: rules + dictionaries + one NER model)

Compiled 2026-10-05. Searched and fetched with WebSearch/WebFetch only. No clinical text was sent anywhere.

**How sure each citation is**
- **[V]**: I fetched the URL and checked the bibliographic details and the key numbers on that page. This covers abstract pages from Europe PMC REST, PMC, arXiv, ACL Anthology, PhysioNet, Zenodo and PMLR.
- **[S]**: The details come only from search-result snippets. The URL showed up in search results but I did not open it, or opening it failed (403/404/captcha). Check before citing.
- Numbers are as stated in the abstract or landing page unless I say otherwise. Where a number came through a summarizer and looks odd, I say so.

## 2. Patient-specific runtime dictionaries and known-identifier matching

| # | Paper | Venue / URL | Finding |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | **Gupta D, Saul M, Gilbertson J. "Evaluation of a deidentification (De-Id) software engine to share pathology reports and clinical documents for research."** 2004 | Am J Clin Pathol 121:176–186. [S: citation only; full text not checked] | An early commercial-academic engine (De-Id) using rules, dictionaries and header-derived patient data. I could not confirm details. |
| 2.2 | **Beckwith BA, Mahaadevan R, Balis UJ, Kuo F. "Development and evaluation of an open source software tool for deidentification of pathology reports."** 2006 | BMC Med Inform Decis Mak 6:12. DOI 10.1186/1472-6947-6-12. [V via Europe PMC] https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1421388/ | HMS Scrubber works in three steps: **(1) match known patient identifiers, (2) regex for predictable identifiers, (3) proper-name and geographic dictionaries.** On 1,800 pathology reports it removed 98.3% of identifiers, missing 19 HIPAA identifiers. |
| 2.3 | **Friedlin FJ, McDonald CJ. "A software tool for removing patient identifying information from clinical documents" (MeDS).** 2008 | JAMIA 15(5):601–610. DOI 10.1197/jamia.M2702. [V via Europe PMC] | Works on HL7 messages. It scrubbed 99.47% of HIPAA identifiers in 7,190 pathology messages, and about 95% of output stayed readable. The abstract does not describe how it uses the HL7 PID/patient fields. My recollection that MeDS uses identifiers from the message's patient segments is **unverified**. |
| 2.4 | **Dehghan et al. 2015** (as in 1.7) | J Biomed Inform 58:S53. [V for bibliography; mechanism from search snippet] | **Two-pass approach:** the first pass finds high-confidence PHI. Those strings form a **patient-specific run-time dictionary**, which the second pass uses to catch mentions that have no contextual cues. This only needs the notes themselves, so it also works when structured EHR fields are not available. |
| 2.5 | **Lee JY, Dernoncourt F, Uzuner Ö, Szolovits P. "Feature-augmented neural networks for patient note de-identification."** 2016 | COLING ClinicalNLP workshop. [V] https://arxiv.org/abs/1610.09704 | Adding **EHR-derived features** (known patient names and similar) to an ANN improved on the state of the art, especially for patient names. The authors recommend using structured EHR data because "in a typical, real-life setting" notes come with it. |
| 2.6 | **Fernandes AC, Cloete D, Broadbent MT, ... Callard F. "Development and evaluation of a de-identification procedure for a case register sourced from mental health electronic records" (CRIS, South London & Maudsley).** 2013 | BMC Med Inform Decis Mak 13:71. DOI 10.1186/1472-6947-13-71. [V via Europe PMC full text] | **A purely patient-specific dictionary approach.** Identifiers come from structured fields (patient and family names, NHS number, address, DOB), are expanded to format variants, and are masked wherever they appear in free text. **P 98.8% / R 97.6% on 500 notes.** In a 20-note comparison against MIST it scored P 100% / R 88.5%: perfect precision, but it misses PHI not present in the structured fields. |
| 2.7 | **Neamatullah 2008** (1.2) and **Norgeot 2020** (1.11) | | Both rely on large static lists: name and location lexicons as blacklists, and medical-term whitelists or safe lists to suppress false positives. Philter's ~195k-term UMLS/SNOMED/MeSH whitelist is the main thing that keeps precision workable at 99%+ recall. |

Summary: the known-identifier pass is cheap and very precise (about 98–100%), and it is strongest for exactly the most harmful category, the patient's own name, MRN and address. Its recall is limited to identifiers that are in the structured record, so a general detector is still needed for clinician names, third parties, free-typed dates and the like. I found no peer-reviewed paper describing a Datavant-style tokenization approach for notes.

## 3. ASR, spoken and conversational PHI detection

| # | Paper | Venue / URL | Finding |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | **Cohn I, Laish I, Beryozkin G, Li G, Shafran I, Szpektor I, Hartman T, Hassidim A, Matias Y. "Audio De-identification: A New Entity Recognition Task."** 2019 | NAACL-HLT 2019 Industry, pp. 197–204. [V: full PDF read] https://aclanthology.org/N19-2025/ | Pipeline of ASR, text NER and alignment back to the audio. Key numbers: **WER on PHI words was 41.8%** (38.3% on non-PHI). **A NER model trained on i2b2 2014 had recall of only 0.37 on conversational transcripts**, against 0.82–0.88 for in-domain models. End-to-end F1 was only about 0.5. In error analysis, **45% of false negatives came from ASR transcription errors and 50% from NER errors**. Augmenting training data with case and ASR noise helped end-to-end results. Taking the OR across ASR n-best hypotheses helped only slightly. |
| 3.2 | **Pasad A, Wu F, Shon S, Livescu K, Han KJ. "On the Use of External Data for Spoken Named Entity Recognition."** 2022 | NAACL 2022. [V] https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.07648 | A pipeline (ASR → text NER) is the strongest baseline. End-to-end models win only when external data is used. For a small system, the pipeline remains the practical choice. |
| 3.3 | **Blau Y, Agrawal R, ... Ramabhadran B. "Using Text Injection to Improve Recognition of Personal Identifiers in Speech."** 2023 | Interspeech 2023 (venue per arXiv comments; I saw only the arXiv page, so treat the venue as **[S]**). [V] https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.07393 | Training ASR on de-identified medical speech hurts recognition of PII. Injecting fake text PII during training raised **name recall by 8% and date recall by 13%** on medical data. ASR trained on redacted corpora under-recognizes the PHI you most need to catch. |
| 3.4 | **Ellis Z, Joselowitz J, Deo Y, ... Lim E. "WER is Unaware: Assessing How ASR Errors Distort Clinical Understanding in Patient Facing Dialogue."** 2026 | IWSDS 2026. [V] https://aclanthology.org/2026.iwsds-1.39.pdf | WER does not predict clinically meaningful distortion of entities. You need entity-level evaluation on ASR output, not WER. |
| 3.5 | **Colonel J, Davidson A, Cecchi G, Lin B. "Evaluating Open-Weight Audio Models for Privacy Verification in Clinical Speech Redaction."** 2026 | ICML 2026 Workshop "Learning to Listen" (workshop). [V] https://icml.cc/virtual/2026/77321 | Treats voice as an identifier. A transcript baseline (Whisper + Gemma) had recall 0.978 but precision 0.316, while the WavLM-ECAPA speaker-verification model had the best F1, 0.564. Verifying speaker redaction is still immature. |
| 3.6 | **Kaiser E, Heiniger N. "Offline Multimodal AI Redaction of Sensitive Data in Audio and Software Artifacts."** 2025 | OST Ostschweizer Fachhochschule student project; not peer reviewed. [S] https://eprints.ost.ch/id/eprint/1369/ | Faster-Whisper + **Presidio** + custom regex, fully offline. Reported PII recall was 97% on text and 99% on transcribed audio, on its own small test set. Weak evidence, but it is the closest match to this project's stack. |

Gap: I found **no peer-reviewed benchmark of PHI detection on real clinical-conversation ASR transcripts** with public data. The closest are Cohn 2019, which used Switchboard/Fisher plus a private medical set, and work on de-identified doctor-patient corpora that isn't public.

## 4. Residual-risk and leakage-focused evaluation, not F1 alone

| # | Paper | Venue / URL | Finding |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | **Carrell D, Malin B, Aberdeen J, Bayer S, Clark C, Wellner B, Hirschman L. "Hiding in plain sight: use of realistic surrogates to reduce exposure of protected health information in clinical text."** 2013 | JAMIA 20(2):342–348. DOI 10.1136/amiajnl-2012-001034. [V via Europe PMC] https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3638183 | Every de-id method leaves **residual identifiers**. Replacing detected PHI with realistic surrogates (HIPS) concealed **about 90% of the leaked residual identifiers** in a pilot of 81 notes. |
| 4.2 | **Carrell DS, Malin BA, Cronkite DJ, Aberdeen JS, Clark C, Li MR, Bastakoty D, Nyemba S, Hirschman L. "Resilience of clinical text de-identified with 'hiding in plain sight' to hostile reidentification attacks by human readers."** 2020 | JAMIA 27(9). DOI 10.1093/jamia/ocaa095. [V via Europe PMC] https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7647331 | Four motivated attackers attacked 200 HIPS documents. They found only **26.8% of the leaked PII (precision 37.2%)**: 23% of patient names, 32% of dates, 9% of ages. **Surrogate replacement is a cheap risk reducer** that makes a small system's misses much less exploitable than redaction to "[NAME]". |
| 4.3 | **Osborne JD, Trotter A, ... Kennedy RE. "A Markov Chain Replacement Strategy for Surrogate Identifiers: Minimizing Re-Identification Risk While Preserving Text Reuse."** 2025 | Electronics 14(19):3945. DOI 10.3390/electronics14193945. [V via Europe PMC] https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12536513/ | How you replace PHI matters. With a token false-negative rate of 0.1%, document-level PHI leakage was **27.1% under consistent replacement and 0.1% under their Markov replacement**. At 0.5% FNER it went from 94.2% to 57.7%. Even small token miss rates can leak at the document level if surrogates are applied consistently. |
| 4.4 | **Pilán et al. TAB 2022** (1.22) | [V] | Proposes **entity-level recall**: an entity counts only if *all* its mentions are masked. It is reported **separately for direct identifiers and quasi-identifiers**, alongside weighted token precision. Mention-level recall hid Presidio's failure on direct identifiers. |
| 4.5 | **Norgeot 2020** (1.11); **Johnson 2020** (1.12); **Dernoncourt 2017** (1.8) | | All argue for or use **binary token-level PHI/non-PHI recall** as the main safety metric, since a missed token is a leak whatever its type. Philter reports F2 to weight recall. |
| 4.6 | **Berg H, Henriksson A, Dalianis H. "The Impact of De-identification on Downstream Named Entity Recognition in Clinical Text."** 2020 | LOUHI 2020. [V] https://aclanthology.org/2020.louhi-1.1/ | Utility side: de-id with moderate-to-high precision leaves downstream NER unchanged. Low precision, or deleting whole sentences, harms it. Replacing names with surrogates costs almost nothing. This puts a bound on how much over-redaction you can accept. |
| 4.7 | **Ford E, Pillinger S, Stewart R, Jones K, Roberts A, Casey A, Goddard K, Nenadic G. "What is the patient re-identification risk from using de-identified clinical free text data for health research?"** 2025 | AI and Ethics. DOI 10.1007/s43681-025-00681-0. [V via Europe PMC] https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12449363/ | Review: inside secure data environments, the re-identification risk from de-identified free text is **very low**. Residual risk should be assessed together with the access controls, not from the model alone. |
| 4.8 | **Steinkamp JM, Pomeranz T, Adleberg J, Kahn CE Jr, Cook TS. "Evaluation of Automated Public De-Identification Tools on a Corpus of Radiology Reports."** 2020 | Radiol Artif Intell 2(6). DOI 10.1148/ryai.2020190137. [V via Europe PMC] | Off-the-shelf tools on 1,023 test radiology reports: the best was NeuroNER with F1 92.2%, but **no tool exceeded 50% F1 on patient names**. Aggregate F1 hides failures on the most dangerous category. |

## 5. Confidence calibration for NER and de-identification

| # | Paper | Venue / URL | Finding |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5.1 | **Guo C, Pleiss G, Sun Y, Weinberger KQ. "On Calibration of Modern Neural Networks."** 2017 | ICML 2017, PMLR 70. [S] https://proceedings.mlr.press/v70/guo17a | Classic baseline: modern networks are overconfident. **Temperature scaling**, a single parameter, is a surprisingly effective post-hoc fix. |
| 5.2 | **Desai S, Durrett G. "Calibration of Pre-trained Transformers."** 2020 | EMNLP 2020, pp. 295–302. [S] https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.07892 | BERT/RoBERTa are reasonably calibrated in-domain, and temperature scaling improves this further. **Out of domain, calibration degrades** (label smoothing helps). The tasks were NLI, paraphrase and commonsense, not NER. |
| 5.3 | **Jagannatha A, Yu H. "Calibrating Structured Output Predictors for Natural Language Processing."** 2020 | ACL 2020. [S] https://aclanthology.org/2020.acl-main.188 | **Entity-level** calibration for structured outputs (NER, POS, QA), motivated by healthcare use. It beats token-level and temperature baselines, and the calibrated scores can drive entity-specific decoding. Calibrate per entity span, not per token softmax. |
| 5.4 | **Singer M, Pazdernik K, Sengupta S. "Uncertainty Quantification for Named Entity Recognition via Conformal Prediction."** 2026 | AISTATS 2026, PMLR 300:3178–3186. [V] https://proceedings.mlr.press/v300/singer26a.html | Conformal prediction sets over NER label sequences with finite-sample coverage. Mondrian (per-group) calibration, stratified by length and language, keeps the sets small. Tested on general NER (CoNLL, WikiNEuRal), not clinical data. |
| 5.5 | **Shrestha M, Kim E. "Conformal Prediction for Risk-Controlled Medical Entity Extraction Across Clinical Domains."** 2026 | arXiv 2603.00924 (preprint). [V] https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.00924 | **The direction of miscalibration flips across document types**: underconfident on structured FDA labels, overconfident on free-text radiology, where thresholds up to 0.99 were needed. At least 90% coverage was reached with 9–13% rejection. Calibrate per document type and per entity category, and recalibrate for each new source. |

Gap: I found **no peer-reviewed paper on calibration or conformal methods specifically for clinical PHI de-identification**. The practical takeaway: fit temperature or per-type thresholds on a local held-out set, use split-conformal or per-type recall-targeted thresholds (for example, choose the threshold that achieves at least 99% recall on calibration data), and recalibrate per note type and per modality (typed vs ASR).

## Implications for a minimal system

- **One NER model plus rules plus dictionaries is enough.** The 2014 winner, 2016 N-GRID entries and Spark NLP were all single-model hybrids (CRF or BiLSTM or BERT, plus regex and dictionaries). Ensembles bought only +1.1 to +1.5 F1 and no recall gain (Kim/Heider/Meystre 2020). Philter reached 99%+ recall with almost no ML. One model plus a rule layer is the right size, and a second model is not worth its cost.
- **The rule and dictionary layer is what secures recall.** Hybrid beat NER-only by about 10% (Kocaman 2023). Regex handled dates, phone numbers and IDs (Stubbs 2015). Custom recognizers for local ID formats are essential: TAB showed stock Presidio missed every case number, giving direct-identifier entity recall of about 0.45. Out of the box on clinical notes, Presidio scored P 0.51 / R 0.74, so it cannot be used uncustomized.
- **Add a patient-specific known-identifier pass** built from structured fields (name variants, MRN, DOB formats, address, family names). It is cheap and very precise (CRIS: P 98.8 / R 97.6), and it targets the most harmful PHI. Add a second-pass run-time dictionary that propagates every high-confidence detection to all mentions in the document (Dehghan 2015), because TAB-style entity recall needs *all* mentions masked.
- **Add a medical-term safe list (whitelist).** It is what keeps precision workable at a high-recall operating point (Philter). Over-redaction harms downstream utility (Berg 2020; Aghakasiri 2025).
- **Expect a 10–20 point drop at a new site or note type.** See Yang 2019 (−10 F1), N-GRID sight-unseen (0.80), VA (0.67) and Steinkamp (names under 50%). Budget a local annotated set, with returns flattening around 700 notes (Yang 2020), or at least a local recall audit. Never validate only on synthetic PHI (Yeniterzi 2010, F 0.96 → 0.73). Treat MEDDOCAN scores as optimistic.
- **Expect ASR transcripts to perform very differently from typed notes.** PHI WER was about 42% and an i2b2-trained model's recall fell to 0.37 (Cohn 2019). Ideally include ASR-noised and lower-cased training/augmentation, keep a separate operating threshold for audio, and evaluate entity recall on transcripts, not WER.
- **Use surrogate replacement (HIPS), not "[REDACTED]".** It hides about 70–90% of residual leaks from attackers (Carrell 2013, 2020) at no model cost. Use non-trivial surrogate mapping, because consistent replacement can amplify document-level leakage (Osborne 2025).
- **Report leakage-oriented metrics:** binary token-level PHI recall, entity-level recall with all mentions masked, and recall for direct and quasi identifiers reported separately (patient name, MRN), plus F2. Don't use micro-F1 as the headline number.
- **Calibrate the single model locally.** Use temperature or per-entity-type thresholds fitted to a recall target on a held-out set (split-conformal style), and recalibrate per document type and per modality, because the direction of miscalibration can flip (Shrestha & Kim 2026). There is no clinical-de-id-specific calibration literature, so this has to be validated locally.
- **Data for building and evaluating, legally:** MEDDOCAN (CC BY 4.0) is the only fully open gold standard. CARMEN-I, the PhysioNet deid gold standard and MIMIC require PhysioNet credentialing, CITI training and a DUA (CARMEN-I also needs author approval). i2b2/n2c2 2006/2014 need the DBMI portal DUA. Check each DUA's terms on derived models before shipping any weights.
